Can Debt Review Be Removed or Terminated?

“Removal” means different things at different stages. Here's what actually applies — and why anyone guaranteeing a quick exit for a fee deserves scrutiny.

Published 21 September 2026 · Updated 21 September 2026 · 3 min read

When people ask about “removing debt review” they usually mean one of several quite different things. The honest answer depends entirely on which one applies to you, and on what stage you are at.

What people usually mean

  • “I've finished paying and I want the debt review flag off my credit record.”
  • “I applied, but I've changed my mind and it hasn't gone to court yet.”
  • “My circumstances improved and I can afford my original instalments again.”
  • “I'm already under a court order and I want out.”

If you have completed the process

This is the cleanest route. Once the debts covered by your re-arrangement are satisfied, a registered debt counsellor issues a clearance certificate under section 71 of the National Credit Act, and the detail of the debt review must then be expunged from the credit bureaus' records.

If a court order is already in place

Exiting before the debts are settled is a different matter, and generally requires a court application. Removal follows specific legal routes — an improved income on its own does not automatically qualify you, and there is no administrative shortcut that bypasses the order.

If your circumstances have genuinely changed

A change in income doesn't automatically end debt review, but it is a legitimate reason to have your situation reassessed. What is available to you depends on your stage in the process and what the court order (if any) says.

What the National Credit Regulator said in March 2025

In Circular 2 of 2025, the National Credit Regulator issued a public warning about “debt review removal” services. The circular records that consumers were being asked to pay fees of up to R10 000 upfront, and that in most instances the consumer's debt review status was not removed as claimed. The NCR states that such practices, and the advertising of them, are contrary to the National Credit Act.

The legal point underneath it is specific: under the NCA, a person offering a service to improve a consumer's credit record or remove information from a credit bureau may not receive any payment from that consumer until the service has been fully performed. An upfront fee for promised removal is the thing the regulator is warning about.

How to protect yourself

  • Be sceptical of “guaranteed” or “instant” removal, especially before anyone has looked at your file.
  • Do not pay a large upfront fee for a promised credit-record outcome.
  • Ask which specific legal route applies to your situation — clearance certificate, withdrawal, or a court application.
  • Check that whoever you deal with is a registered debt counsellor on the NCR's public register.
  • Remember that lodging a dispute with a credit bureau is free, and the Credit Ombud's service is free.

So: be sceptical of a guaranteed outcome quoted before anyone has looked at your file, and be especially sceptical of a large fee demanded upfront for that promise.

What an honest answer looks like

Anyone dealing with this properly should ask what stage you are at, whether an order exists, and what your current position with creditors is — before telling you what's possible. If someone tells you removal is simple without asking any of that, they are not describing your actual situation.

Frequently asked

Can I be removed from debt review just because I can afford my payments again?

No. Removal follows specific legal routes and requires an assessment. An improved income alone doesn't automatically qualify you.

Can a company guarantee they'll remove me from debt review?

Treat guarantees with caution. The NCR has warned about businesses promising fast removal for large upfront fees, and s126A(3) of the NCA generally prohibits taking payment before such a service is actually carried out.

What is a clearance certificate?

The document a registered debt counsellor issues once the debts covered by your re-arrangement are paid in full. It's used to update your status with the credit bureaus.
